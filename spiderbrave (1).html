A Note on This Project

I built this before I knew how to document things properly.

SpiderBrave was one of my earlier projects. I was younger, more focused on building than on recording what I was building, and formal documentation was not something I thought about yet. By the time I understood its value, the project was done.

What I can give you here is the honest story of what I made, why I made it that way, and what one real person with arachnophobia told me when they played it. Sometimes that is enough.

This case study is reconstructed from memory and the limited materials that still exist. The thinking behind the design was sound. The documentation was not. I have since learned that those two things need to happen together.

The Research

It started with a literature review, not a game engine.

Before writing a single line of code, I spent time reading existing research on exposure therapy and arachnophobia. Exposure therapy is a well-established psychological treatment that works by gradually and repeatedly exposing a person to the thing they fear, in a controlled environment, until the fear response diminishes.

The key word in that definition is gradual. The research was clear that throwing someone directly into their worst fear does not work and can make things worse. The therapeutic approach depends entirely on a structured, incremental progression. That became the design principle for every decision I made.

The game had to feel safe enough to stay in, but uncomfortable enough to actually work. That is a narrower design target than it sounds.

The Design

Three levels. One direction. No going back.

The entire game takes place in VR. In each level, the player has one task: walk across the room to reach the other side. The spider is between them and the exit. The spider changes with each level. The task does not.

Level 01
First Contact
One cartoon spider, moving slowly across the floor. Visually soft, clearly not real, no threat. The point is just to be in the same room as it and keep walking. Most people manage this.
Level 02
Getting Closer
More realistic spider. Moves faster. Crawls as well as walks. The visual stakes increase. The task is still the same. Walk across the room. But now it requires more deliberate effort to do it.
Level 03
The Real Thing
Multiple spiders. Highly realistic. Moving at something close to actual spider speed. Multiple directions. This is the level where the fear response is real and getting across the room is a genuine achievement.

User Feedback

One person who actually had arachnophobia played it.

I tested SpiderBrave with other students and friends. Most of them found it interesting, occasionally uncomfortable at level three, but manageable. One person in the group actually had arachnophobia.

It was easier to start with the cartoon spider.
A participant with arachnophobia, during informal testing

That one sentence told me the graduated design was working as intended. The cartoon entry point was not a compromise. It was the reason someone with a real phobia was able to engage with the game at all rather than stopping immediately. Starting small is not a lesser version of the experience. It is the whole point.

What I Took Away

Research before development is not optional. It is the brief.

  • The literature review was not background reading. It was the design specification. Every decision cartoon first, single spider first, slow speed first came directly from what the research said about how exposure therapy actually works.
  • Graduated progression is everything in therapeutic design. The jump between levels has to feel achievable at every step, or the whole system fails.
  • The simplest possible task walk across the room turned out to be exactly the right mechanic. It kept the focus on the fear, not on game controls or objectives.
  • Document your work as you go. Not after. Not when you remember. As you go. I learned this the hard way and it directly changed how I approached BioVRium.
